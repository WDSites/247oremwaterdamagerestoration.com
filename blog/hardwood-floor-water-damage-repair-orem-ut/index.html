Hardwood Floor Water Damage Repair in Orem, UT

How cupping happens, how a wet floor gets dried, and how to choose between refinishing and replacing boards.

By the 247 Orem Water Damage Restoration team

Why wet hardwood cups

Wood takes on moisture through its underside faster than through its sealed top. When a dishwasher line leaks or a slab sweats, the bottom of each plank swells while the top stays put, and the board edges curl upward into a shallow trough. That curl is cupping.

The opposite shape is crowning, with the center of a board peaking higher than its edges. It usually shows up after a cupped floor has been sanded while it was still wet. Both conditions mean the wood holds more water than it should, and neither is fixed by finish alone.

Signs the floor is reacting to moisture

  • Board edges that feel raised, with a visible dip across the middle of each plank
  • Gaps opening between boards after they first swelled tight
  • White haze or cloudy patches in the finish
  • Dark stains along seams or around nail heads
  • A hollow sound or soft give when you step near a wall or fixture
  • A floor that lifts off the subfloor at one end

How a wet hardwood floor gets dried

  1. Stop the water and lift what you can

    Shut off the source and remove rugs, furniture, and anything holding water against the boards. Wipe standing water with towels and run a wet vac over the surface. Never drag heavy items across a swollen floor.

  2. Read the moisture

    A moisture meter set for the wood species shows how wet the planks are, and a second reading on the subfloor shows whether water is trapped below. A thermal imaging camera helps find wet zones that the surface hides. These readings are your baseline.

  3. Dry from above and below

    Crews place air movers and LGR (low grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers, and many use floor drying mats that pull moisture through the boards. If the floor sits over a crawl space or basement, drying also happens underneath.

  4. Hold off on heat and sanding

    Cranking up the furnace or aiming a space heater at the floor dries the top too fast and can split boards. Sanding wet wood locks in the cup. Patience here is what saves the floor.

  5. Recheck until readings flatten

    Wood often relaxes back toward flat as it dries over days or weeks. A floor that still cups after readings match a dry reference needs a different plan.

Solid, engineered, and the subfloor underneath

Solid hardwood can often be sanded several times over its life, so a cupped floor that flattens may refinish well. Engineered flooring has a thin veneer over a plywood or fiberboard core. Its wear layer allows only limited sanding, and the core can swell and delaminate when wet.

The subfloor decides the outcome as often as the boards do. Plywood or oriented strand board that stays wet will keep pushing moisture up into the planks. When a crew finds a wet subfloor, repair work waits until it passes a reading and any soft or stained panels are cut out and replaced.

Sanding and refinishing done right

A refinish starts with a three-pass sand, working from coarse to fine grit with an edger for the borders. The crew vacuums, tacks the dust off, applies stain if the owner wants one, then lays two or three coats of finish with drying time between coats. Rushing coats traps solvent and causes soft spots.

Boards that were replaced need to match the old floor in species, width, and grade, then get blended at the seams with the surrounding finish. Matching the sheen over a whole room often means refinishing wall to wall instead of patching one corner.

Mistakes that cost homeowners their floor

Sealing it in

Covering wet boards with a rug, new finish, or plastic locks moisture in and speeds mold growth under the covering.

Sanding early

A floor sanded while wet will crown as it dries, which means a second sanding or replacement.

Guessing by eye

A floor that looks flat on top can still read wet underneath, so meter readings matter more than appearance.

Skipping the source

A slow fixture leak, appliance line, or damp slab will cup the next floor too if nobody traces it.

What to document for repairs and claims

Photograph the floor in raking light, with a lamp low to the surface, so the cup shows in the image. Record the room, the plank width, and the species if you know it. Keep the installer invoice or product details, since they help a contractor match the replacement.

Save moisture readings from before and after drying and keep any board pieces that were removed. These records help an adjuster see whether the damage came from a sudden event or a slow leak, which affects coverage. Ask your insurer what it wants to see.

When floor repair needs a professional

Call when water has covered more than a few square feet, when boards are buckling, or when the floor sits over a finished basement ceiling. Fast water mitigation is what keeps a floor that can be saved from becoming a floor that cannot. Once it is dry, water damage repair covers the sanding, board replacement, and finish.

Our dispatch also reaches homes in Pleasant Grove and American Fork, where older homes with original oak or maple floors face the same decisions. We answer 24/7, and while you wait you can lift rugs and wipe the surface.

Drying targets and the order of work for wet flooring follow ANSI/IICRC S500, the published standard for water damage restoration. It is worth asking any company you hire whether its crews work to it, and what drying readings they will show you.
